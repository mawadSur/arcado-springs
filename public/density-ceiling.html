The density ceiling

Parcel 4535–4541 Arcado Rd SW, Lilburn GA Area 417,173.6 sf · 9.44 ac deeded Shape 234.7 ft × 1,721 ft ribbon Updated 20 Sep 2026

The question asked eight different ways was: can this site hold sixty homes? The answer is no — 44 is the ceiling for a 55+ detached cottage community, and 41 is what full engineering closure actually produces. This page shows every lever in the Lilburn ordinance, tested and priced.

But the ninth attempt changed something else. Shortening the lot to 50 × 80 ft costs no homes at all and frees 74.7 ft of cross-section — enough for a linear green and a walking trail the full length of the site. That is the proposal on this page.

One equation governs everything

On a ribbon this narrow there is room for exactly one road with one row of lots on each side. So the number of homes is not a design question. It is division.

homes = usable lane frontage ÷ minimum lot width

NE side   1,754 gross
  −100  entry drive + curb returns
  −180  three hammerhead legs
  − 30  curb returns at T-intersections
  = 1,444 ft

SW side   1,754 gross
  −100  entry drive
  −500  stream screen (75-ft setback + 5-ft top-of-bank)
  −180  three hammerhead legs
  −180  one detention basin outside the screened reach
  = 794 ft

(1,444 + 794) ÷ 50 = 44.8 → 44 homes

Every one of those deductions is a physical object that has to sit somewhere along the lane. The two detention basins are 180 ft long apiece and they are not optional — 75,410 cf required against 77,863 cf provided is a 3.3 % margin with no slack.

“The lot count follows the drainage design, not the other way round.” — docs/03, Letter of Intent

Why there is only one road

Table 4.1 sets a minimum lot depth of 100 ft for all uses in R-2. Two rows of lots consume 200 ft of a ribbon that measures 234.7 ft at the right-of-way. What is left over is the entire circulation budget.

That 31-ft tract is the governing minimum: 22 ft of pavement, a 5-ft sidewalk, and 2 ft of curb-and-utility strip either side. It carries one sidewalk. Two sidewalks need 36 ft, which the ribbon only reaches around station 1,080.

The section is also not uniform. The boundary measures 234.70 ft at the Arcado Road right-of-way and does not reach 236.00 ft until station 254. The front of the site works only because the amenity block — clubhouse, village green, courts, mail kiosk, guest parking — occupies it, and lots do not begin until station 230.

The proposal: 50 × 80

Lot depth appears nowhere in the yield equation, which is why every earlier study treated it as worthless. That was the mistake. Depth is the only variable that frees cross-section, and on a ribbon this narrow cross-section is the scarce thing — not homes.

Taking 20 ft off the depth and spending none of it on width turns a 34.7-ft circulation budget into a 74.7-ft one, at no cost in homes: 43 either way.

Why this variance is strong where the last one was fatal

Concept F asked to shorten the lot to 82 ft and was killed on self-created hardship: a conforming 100-ft lot demonstrably fits, so asking for relief to get something nicer fails §1005. At 50 × 80 the answer to “what do you gain?” is nothing — 43 homes either way. The request is not for density. It is to let a 234.7-ft ribbon hold a central green that its own exceptional narrowness otherwise forecloses, which is hardship arising from the shape of the land. Asking for no advantage is the best posture a variance application can have.

Status of this number

The 43 is arithmetic — usable frontage divided by lot width — not a fit. It has not yet been modelled against the boundary ring, the stream screen, grades, sewer inverts or the detention budget, and shortening the lots moves the disturbed-area calculation. Treat it as a direction, not a plan, until it closes.

The one route to sixty, and what it costs

Table 4.1 sets the minimum lot width for a fee-simple townhouse in R-2 at 20 ft, on a 2,000-sf lot. At that module the 2,238 ft of usable frontage yields well past sixty, and the 8 du/ac cap (75 homes) becomes the binding limit rather than the geometry.

It is a real option. It is not the option you are building.

  • Special Use Permit required. §602 codes Single-family Attached (fee simple) as S in R-2 — a separate public hearing and Council approval on top of the rezoning.
  • Rear entry is mandatory. §734-2(2)(b): “All townhomes must be rear entry,” with an 18-ft alley and garages 20 ft off it. Two rows plus an alley plus buffers is 258 ft — it does not fit. Only a single row does.
  • 1,400 sf minimum heated area for all dwelling types other than cottage homes, against 1,000 sf for a cottage — which forces two storeys and breaks the one-story, 24-ft-ridge commitment.
  • Three to eight units per row (§734-2(1)(a)), each row needing alternating rooflines and facade treatments.
  • It is no longer a 55+ detached cottage community — which is the product, the market comparables, the HOPA structure and every drawing in the package.

The honest headline

Forty-four is the ceiling for the project you are building. Sixty exists only by building a different one.

What the shared-green idea can actually be

A continuous shared backyard behind the lots is barred three separate ways, any one of them fatal: the review checklist §6.f allows buffer encroachments only “as near as perpendicular as possible” — a 1,740-ft trail running along the buffer is its opposite; the HOA covenants already prohibit mowing to turf, grading, and “any impervious surface, walkway, paver, gravel or artificial turf” there; and grading those two strips moves 73,744 sf from preserved to disturbed, pushing required detention from 75,410 cf to roughly 92,340 cf against 77,863 cf provided.

But the plan already owns the ground to do it properly — 141,089 sf of open space, 33.8 % of the site, in eight tracts:

The buildable version of the reference images

Without a variance: a porous-paved loop linking amenity → pocket green → pocket green → terminus green → creek woods, with porches, walks and shared seating turned onto the two pocket greens. Cottage-court character on ground already committed to open space, at 41–44 homes. The HOA outline already assigns “Trail or seating in the greens” to the covenants; it has simply never been drawn.

With the depth variance: the 50 × 80 proposal replaces the three discrete pocket greens with one continuous 34.7-ft linear green running the full 1,754 ft, trail on both edges, every home fronting it. Same 43 homes. That is the stronger answer to the brief, and it is the one worth taking to a pre-application conference.

One document left

Development Regulations Appendix B — the private-street standard the review checklist §4.s binds this plan to. The project record marks it not retrievable. Every road dimension on this page, including the 31-ft minimum and the 20-ft one-way lanes, is a reconstruction of a standard no one on the project has seen. It is the first question for the Planning Director.

The second open item is now closed. §602’s note about “development standards for minimum unit size and lot configurations” attaches to the Single-family Detached row, not to cluster-cottage — and no such standard exists for cottages anywhere in the ordinance. The word “cottage” appears five times in all 172 pages: the R-2 purpose sentence, three Table 4.1 rows, and the use-table row. Article 7 runs 701–729 with no cottage section. Table 4.1 is the entire standard, which is why any depth under 100 ft is a §1005 variance and nothing else.
